Readability scores explained: Flesch-Kincaid, Gunning Fog & what to target

June 8, 2026 6 min read By WrdKit Team

Readability scores are formulas that estimate how easy — or difficult — a piece of text is to read. They matter for SEO, content marketing, academic writing, and UX copy. But most writers misread what the scores mean, or target the wrong numbers for their audience. Here's the complete guide.

Why readability matters

Google's quality rater guidelines explicitly value content that is "easy to read and understand." Studies consistently show that online readers scan rather than read — they bail on dense, complex paragraphs within seconds. Readable content means lower bounce rates, longer time on page, and higher conversion rates. It also directly affects SEO performance.

For academic and medical writing, readability scores help ensure documents are accessible to their intended audience — a patient information leaflet written at a university level is a usability failure, regardless of how accurate it is.

The Flesch Reading Ease score

The Flesch Reading Ease formula, developed by Rudolf Flesch in 1948, produces a score from 0 to 100. Higher = easier to read. It's the most widely used readability formula in the world.

The formula factors in average sentence length and average syllable count per word.

ScoreDifficultyTypical Content
90–100Very EasyChildren's books, simple instructions
80–90EasyConversational English, SMS
70–80Fairly EasyConsumer blogs, tabloid newspapers
60–70StandardMost online articles, this guide
50–60Fairly DifficultQuality newspapers, professional reports
30–50DifficultAcademic journals, legal documents
0–30Very DifficultTechnical papers, scientific writing
Target for most web content: Aim for a Flesch Reading Ease score of 60–70. This is the "standard" range — readable without being condescending. Blog posts, landing pages, and email marketing all perform best in this range.

The Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level

The Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level converts the same measurement into a US school grade level. A score of 8 means the content is readable by an 8th grader. A score of 12 means it requires a high school diploma. A score of 16 maps to a college senior.

Grade LevelReading LevelRecommended For
Grade 611–12 years oldMass-market consumer content
Grade 813–14 years oldGeneral online articles, most blogs
Grade 1015–16 years oldQuality journalism, B2B content
Grade 1217–18 years oldProfessional reports, whitepapers
Grade 14–16College levelAcademic writing, legal content

For most blogs and marketing content, Grade 7–9 is the sweet spot. This isn't about dumbing down — it's about removing unnecessary complexity. Even highly educated readers prefer clear, direct language when reading online.

Gunning Fog Index

The Gunning Fog Index, developed by Robert Gunning in 1952, measures readability based on sentence length and the percentage of "complex words" (words with three or more syllables).

Like Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level, the Fog score maps roughly to US school years. A score of 12 means a 12th-grade reading level. A score of 17+ is considered college graduate level.

Fog IndexReading Level
66th grade — very accessible
88th grade — ideal for general audiences
10–12High school — quality newspapers
14–16College — academic writing
17+Graduate — extremely dense prose

The Wall Street Journal historically targets a Fog Index of around 11–12. Time magazine targets 10. Most successful online content sits between 8 and 12.

SMOG Index

SMOG (Simple Measure of Gobbledygook) is widely used in healthcare communications because it's validated against real comprehension studies. It measures the number of polysyllabic words (3+ syllables) across a sample of 30 sentences.

For patient-facing health content, health authorities typically recommend a SMOG score of 6 or below — roughly a 6th-grade reading level. This ensures that people with lower literacy levels, or those reading in a second language, can understand critical health information.

How to improve your readability score

You don't need to write like a children's book author to score well. These targeted changes move the needle the most:

Shorten your sentences

The single biggest driver of poor readability scores is long sentences. If your average sentence is over 20 words, your score will suffer. Aim for 15–17 words on average. Vary sentence length — a short sentence after a long one creates rhythm and prevents monotony.

Swap complex words for simpler ones

  • "Utilise" → "use"
  • "Demonstrate" → "show"
  • "Approximately" → "about"
  • "Subsequently" → "then" or "after"
  • "Facilitate" → "help" or "enable"

Break up long paragraphs

Online readers scan. A paragraph of 5+ sentences looks like a wall of text on mobile. Keep paragraphs to 2–3 sentences for web content. Use subheadings every 200–300 words so readers can navigate.

Use active voice

Passive voice adds syllables and obscures who is doing what. "The report was submitted by the team" → "The team submitted the report." Active voice is shorter, clearer, and scores better on readability formulas.

Quick win: Run your draft through a readability checker after writing, then search for your longest sentences. Splitting or simplifying just the top 5 longest sentences will often move your Flesch score by 5–10 points.
