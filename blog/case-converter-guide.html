UPPERCASE vs Title Case vs Sentence case — when to use each in writing

June 8, 2026 5 min read By WrdKit Team

Capitalization is one of those things that seems trivial until you get it wrong — and then it looks unprofessional immediately. This guide covers every major text case convention, when to use each one, and the style guide rules that govern them.

Sentence case

Sentence case capitalizes only the first word of the sentence and any proper nouns. It reads naturally — exactly like a sentence. It's the default for body text in virtually all English writing.

Use sentence case for: article body text, email body, blog post paragraphs, social media captions, UI microcopy (buttons, labels, tooltips), subheadings in conversational content, and most online writing.

Google and most major tech companies (Stripe, Linear, Notion) use sentence case for their UI labels and headings. It feels modern and accessible. Many style guides — including AP Style for digital media — now recommend sentence case for subheadings.

Title Case

Title Case capitalizes the first letter of most words. The "most" is where it gets complicated — every major style guide has slightly different rules about which words to capitalize.

Which words are capitalized in Title Case?

Capitalize: nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, and the first and last word of the title regardless of part of speech.

Do not capitalize (unless first or last word): articles (a, an, the), short prepositions (in, on, at, for, to, with), coordinating conjunctions (and, but, or, nor, yet, so, for).

Style guide differences: AP Style doesn't capitalize prepositions of 4+ letters. Chicago Style capitalizes all prepositions of 4+ letters. APA capitalizes all words of 4+ letters. When in doubt, use a title case converter tool to apply the right style guide consistently.

Use Title Case for: article and blog post titles (H1), book and film titles, formal report titles, press release headlines, and newspaper article headlines.

UPPERCASE

All-caps text communicates urgency, importance, or is used for acronyms and abbreviations. It's one of the most abused cases in writing.

Use UPPERCASE for: acronyms (NASA, FBI, SEO, API), warning labels and critical alerts, legal document section headers (in some jurisdictions), brand names that are stylized in caps (IBM, CNN, ESPN).

Avoid UPPERCASE for: regular emphasis (use bold instead), long passages of text (extremely hard to read), email subject lines (triggers spam filters and looks aggressive), social media posts (reads as shouting).

All-lowercase text is standard for URLs, file names, and some brand identities that have chosen lowercase as their style (tumblr, flickr in their early branding, and many startups today).

Use lowercase for: URLs and slugs, file names, CSS class names, email addresses, some code contexts, and brands that officially style their name lowercase.

Code naming conventions: camelCase, snake_case, kebab-case

In programming, case conventions are non-negotiable — they're part of the language's or framework's standard. Mixing conventions in a codebase is a sign of inconsistency and makes code harder to read.

  • camelCase — JavaScript variables, functions, JSON keys: getUserData(), isLoggedIn
  • PascalCase — JavaScript classes, React components: UserProfile, NavBar
  • snake_case — Python variables and functions, database column names: user_name, created_at
  • SCREAMING_SNAKE_CASE — Constants in most languages: MAX_RETRY_COUNT, API_BASE_URL
  • kebab-case — CSS classes, HTML data attributes, URL slugs: .nav-link, data-user-id

Blog post headline capitalization: Title Case or Sentence case?

This is the most debated question in content publishing, and both are acceptable — but pick one and be consistent across your entire site.

Title Case: More formal, traditional. Preferred by newspapers, magazines, and publishers. Works well for authority content, long-form guides, and sites aiming for a journalistic tone.

Sentence case: More modern, conversational, accessible. Used by most tech companies and SaaS blogs. Feels less corporate. Works well for consumer content, tutorials, and opinion pieces.

WrdKit uses sentence case for subheadings and Title Case for H1 article titles — a common hybrid approach that balances formality with readability.
